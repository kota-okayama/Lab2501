May 20, 2024ABSTRACT
Active learning is designed to minimize annotation efforts by prioritizing instances that most enhance learning. However, many active learning strategies struggle with a ‘cold start’ problem, needing substantial initial data to be effective. This limitation often reduces their utility for pre-trained models, which already perform well in few-shot scenarios. To address this, we introduce ActiveLLM, a novel active learning approach that leverages large language models such as GPT-4, Llama 3, and
Mistral Large for selecting instances. We demonstrate that ActiveLLM significantly enhances the classification performance of BERT classifiers in few-shot scenarios, outperforming both traditional active learning methods and the few-shot learning method SetFit. Additionally, ActiveLLM can be extended to non-few-shot scenarios, allowing for iterative selections. In this way, ActiveLLM can even help other active learning strategies to overcome their cold start problem. Our results suggest that ActiveLLM offers a promising solution for improving model performance across various learning setups.
Keywords Active Learning ·Large Language Models ·Few-Shot Learning ·Cold Start Problem
1 Introduction
The selection of training examples significantly impacts the performance of models. Even LLMs, such as GPT-3, show high variances based on training example selection [ 35]. This dependency is even more pronounced in smaller models such as BERT [ 35], which are sometimes preferred for their cost and resource efficiency as well as data protection benefits over APIs. Therefore, we explore the classical concept of active learning for BERT-like models, enhanced by foundation models like GPT-4 [18].
Active learning encompasses strategies integrated into the labeling process to select instances with the highest learning impact [ 36]. Typically, a model is trained iteratively during the annotation process to query instances for labeling by the annotators (oracle). While active learning can yield significant learning improvements with mid-sized datasets, it often encounters a ‘cold start’ problem [ 5], rendering it unsuitable for few-shot learning. The cold start problem occurs when the active learning method cannot make meaningful decisions at the beginning of data labeling due to insufficient data. For example, in the uncertainty strategy, the method aims to select the most uncertain instances based on an iteratively learned classifier during annotation. The incremental classifier cannot make accurate uncertainty guesses without enough data. This problem intensifies as pre-trained models, like BERT [ 6], perform exceptionally well in few-shot scenarios, making active learning unnecessary for many cases. Additionally, iteratively training such a deep learning model during querying is often impractical due to high waiting times. Even in ‘model mismatch scenarios’, where the instance selection model (query model) differs from the model used for final tasks (successor model), active learning can yield limited gains [36].
To address these challenges in few-shot and model mismatch scenarios, we propose ActiveLLM, an active learning method leveraging large language models (LLMs). ActiveLLM can estimate uncertainty and diversity even withoutarXiv:2405.10808v1 [cs.CL] 17 May 2024

2 Related Work
2.1 Foundations
2.1.1 Few-Shot Learning
Few-shot learning refers to scenarios where models must learn from a limited number of training instances. This is particularly challenging in fields where acquiring large datasets is difficult or expensive. While very large lanuage models such as GPT-3 inherently have robust few-shot or zero-shot capabilities, our focus is on enhancing few-shot learning in smaller models such as BERT, as detailed in the introduction. With specific methods these smaller language models can attain similar or even better few-shot performance [26].
One approach to this is to formulate tasks as cloze-style tests, where certain words in a text instance are missing and the language model is employed to fill in the blanks. Task instances are often rephrased into questions so that the answer word to fill corresponds to the label. This way no classifier needs to be trained on top of the language model leading to a more effective way of utilizing the language models [ 9]. This kind of approach is exemplified by Gao et al. [9], Zhang et al. [33], , Schick and Schütze [20], and Tam et al. [26].
The state-of-the-art technique for BERT-like models, known as SetFit [ 28], overcomes the necessity to formulate such cloze-tasks. In SetFit a sentence transformer [ 19] is fine-tuned on the available training data. The instances are then encoded with the resulting sentence transformer and a classification head is trained on them. At inference, the instances are encoded by the fine-tuned sentence transformer and then classified by the trained classification head.
2.1.2 Active Learning
The rationale behind active learning is that a system selectively chooses instances to be labeled, thereby reducing the labeling effort [23]. In this context, annotators serve as oracles, responding to queries from the active learning system regarding the labels of specific instances. This system utilizes a query strategy that typically hinges on measures of uncertainty or diversity. Traditionally, the querying is performed by the machine learning model itself, which undergoes iterative training during the labeling process. For instance, the model might frequently request labels for instances where its uncertainty is highest, measured through approaches like Least Confidence (LC), Margin of Confidence, or entropy.
However, the scope of active learning has expanded to encompass scenarios where the querying model may differ from the final training model—a phenomenon known as model mismatch—or situations where no model is used for querying.
The former scenario is increasingly relevant, as modern models often demand substantial computational resources and time, rendering them impractical for iterative training during labeling. Alternatively, the latter approach often focuses on maximizing the diversity of the selected examples and might involve techniques such as data clustering.
While active learning can significantly streamline the learning process by reducing labeling efforts, most strategies encounter a cold start problem. In the absence of a sufficient initial labeled dataset (label seed), a model may struggle to make informed predictions about uncertainty or diversity [ 32]. This challenge is pronounced when dealing with pre-trained models, which generally require fewer data instances initially. Moreover, these models often necessitate substantial time resources, thereby increasing the necessity for model mismatch scenarios. In the subsequent sections, we give an overview of active learning for pre-trained transformer models, distinguishing between model matching and mismatching scenarios.

2.2 Active Learning with Transformer Models
2.2.1 Emergence
The study by Ein-Dor et al. [7]is among the first to explore active learning with pre-trained transformer models such as BERT. The authors employ well-known active learning strategies, including LC, Core-Set, and Discriminative
Active Learning (D-AL), and integrate these strategies incrementally without model mismatch. They demonstrate that active learning can enhance the performance of BERT classifiers, particularly in low-data scenarios and when the initial dataset (seed set) is rich in relevant class instances. Similarly, Jacobs et al. [13] investigate the use of hybrid active learning strategies combining uncertainty-based and diversity methods, utilizing SentenceBERT embeddings to refine the instance selection process. Their findings indicate that while active learning reduces labeling efforts, its effectiveness with BERT models is mixed, and the improvements are modest compared to older NLP models. With
ALPS, Yuan et al. [32] propose a novel approach to address the cold start problem in active learning by leveraging
BERT’s pre-existing masked language modeling objective along with clustering to select instances. They hypothesize that this strategy provides more reliable confidence scores in the initial stages of model training than a classifier head.
However, the results are mixed which is also later confirmed by Nguyen et al. [17]. Schröder et al. [21] focus on active learning with BERT and DistilRoBERTa, evaluating both models in settings where the query and successor models match. They report improvements using strategies such as Prediction Entropy (PE), Breaking Ties, LC, and Contrastive
Active Learning, though they note that random sampling occasionally outperforms these strategies during early training phases due to the cold start problem. In another development, Seo et al. [22] introduce BATL, an active learning method designed specifically for pre-trained transformer models. This method incorporates triplet loss to account for uncertainty and diversity, drawing on the embeddings and task-specific outputs of the classifier head, and generally surpasses traditional active learning methods. Grießhaber et al. [10] also examine the impact of active learning on
BERT, focusing on the BALD by Houlsby et al. [11] strategy with Monte Carlo Dropout (MCDO). Their results are mixed, sometimes outperforming random sampling in terms of performance and variance, while at other times showing negligible benefits. They further explore the potential of freezing layers within the BERT model to enhance training efficiency and performance in scenarios with limited data. Although this is not discussed in the study presented, it may also be a viable approach for enhancing the efficiency of querying with the same model. As noted by Schröder et al. [21], the incremental training of these models can be time-consuming, which may negate the cost savings from reduced labeling efforts. Moreover, the training of LLMs is not only a time-consuming process but requires significant resources and hyperparameter tuning. The outcomes of this process can vary significantly. Thus, subsequent studies explore mismatch scenarios where the query model differs from the successor model.
2.2.2 Model Mismatch Scenario
In [24], the authors explore active learning for sequence classification using transformer models. They investigate several active learning strategies including BALD and Variation Ratio (VR). For the transformer models, they evaluate the standard implementation of MCDO, as well as an efficient variant where Dropout is applied only to the last layer of the model. Their experiments demonstrate significant improvements in active learning when employed with BERT, distilBERT, and ELECTRA, particularly in scenarios without model mismatch. Additional experiments with varying query and successor models reveal that model mismatches tend to reduce the benefits of active learning. This observation aligns with findings by Lowell et al. [15], who note that mismatch scenarios might lead to worse outcomes than random sampling. However, Shelmanov et al. [24] discover that employing a distilled version of BERT as the query model does not affect performance, which can be attributed to the similar uncertainties between distilled and original models.
Building on these insights, Tsvigun et al. [27] attempt to eliminate the dependence on a specific query model based on the successor model. They do this by using the trained successor model from active learning sessions with distilled versions to pseudolabel the entire dataset, subsequently training an arbitrary model on these instances. Additionally, to enhance efficiency during the iterative training steps in active learning, the authors suggest avoiding predictions on instances where uncertainty was low in previous iterations. Their experiments with uncertainty-based methods such as
Least Confidence, Maximum Normalized Log-Probability (MNLP), and Mahalanobis Distance demonstrate that the pseudo-labeling approach produces results akin to those in non-model mismatch scenarios. However, this approach still requires a reasonably sized distilled model to be employed throughout the active learning process. This model must be iteratively trained and then used for inference. Moreover, the authors emphasize that the performance of the successor model should not be considerably inferior to that of the final model.
In a related effort, Nguyen et al. [17] aim to enhance the findings of Shelmanov et al. [24]. by proposing an iterative training of the successor model concurrently with the annotation of new instances. They use a smaller query model for immediate feedback, which is trained with newly annotated data and distilled insights from the previous iteration’s successor model. While this approach shows some improvement over scenarios that do not incorporate the successor model iteratively, the query model is still a distilled version of the successor model in their experiments.

2.3 Research Gap
The most significant gap in active learning research is the use of LLMs, such as GPT-4, which, to our knowledge, has not been explored previously. However, the use of these models allows us to address additional gaps in the research:
While Ein-Dor et al. [7]and Grießhaber et al. [10] assess the performance of active learning with BERT in low-resource scenarios, their datasets are still considerably larger than those in few-shot scenarios. BERT models tend to achieve very good results even with limited data, which presents a major challenge in active learning with current models, as it only excels when provided with sufficient data (cold start problem). When combining BERT-like models with efficient few-shot learning methods, such as SetFit or ADAPET [ 26], it stands to reason that there might be little or no improvement through active learning. This work aims to overcome this challenge by proposing an active learning strategy that mitigates the cold start problem, enabling improvements also in few-shot scenarios.
Moreover, many studies [ 2,21,24] report that a model mismatch between the query and successor model often leads to unsatisfactory results [ 36]. This is particularly problematic for the practical application of active learning, as the training runtimes for large pre-trained models can be prohibitively long [ 36]. While efforts by Shelmanov et al. [24] and Nguyen et al. [17] attempt to address this by using smaller, similar models for querying, there remains a dependency on the model type. Similarly, Tsvigun et al. [27] offer a promising approach for decoupling the query and successor models, although a distilled version is still required in the process. In contrast to using smaller, resource-efficient models for querying, we propose the use of much larger foundation models. This approach may initially seem counterintuitive due to concerns about high runtimes, but since these models are capable of zero-shot learning, no training is required.
Furthermore, these models can be utilized via several cost-free chat interfaces in real-time and without extensive machine learning expertise, making them ideally suited for practical applications.
3 Method
ActiveLLM is a pool-based sampling method that operates in batch mode, meaning that it selects a subset from a pool of unlabeled data for querying an oracle. This method is particularly suited for scenarios involving model mismatch. It employs instruction-tuned LLMs as query models, while allowing the choice of a successor model to be independent of these models. ActiveLLM does not train the instruction-tuned LLMs during the active learning process, enabling direct application to the unlabeled dataset without encountering the cold-start problem.
The design of prompts is critical for achieving optimal results with instruction-tuned LLMs. To this end, we have crafted detailed prompts in various configurations to determine their effectiveness in this context. We propose two main variants of ActiveLLM: one that does not incorporate feedback, addressing few-shot learning scenarios, and another that does incorporate feedback, suitable for general scenarios involving iterative querying.
3.1 No Feedback Mode: Few-Shot Learning
The prompts are constructed so that the language model receives a description of active learning, including exact details of its tasks, while also keeping the context length small, as this is a limiting factor for current models. In all cases, given a specific task, ActiveLLM creates a prompt consisting of active learning role allocation, instructions on the selection process, a description of how to format the output, followed by a batch of unlabeled instances. The active learning role allocation positions the language model as an active learning component, explaining that it should select instances for a human annotator to label. Then, the instructions on the selection process specify how many instances it should select.
The output format should be a simple list of the indices of the selected instances.
The final prompt design is illustrated in Figure 1. The parameters displayed on the right indicate certain text phrase constellations which can be included, excluded, or adjusted:
•Guidelines: This includes a phrase indicating that the model is given not only a set of instances but also guidelines for the task. Before listing the instances, the guidelines are included.
•Instances: This parameter determines how many instances the model should label. While in the few-shot experiments this parameter is set to 32, we also test different values in Section 4.2.3.
•Advice: Initial experiments showed that the LLMs often respond with explaining general strategies for choosing instances in an active learning manner. Hence, we included the parameter ‘advice,’ which incorporates these strategies (in the figure, this is abbreviated, but we included the full prompt in Appendix A Figure 7)

3.2 Feedback Mode: Iterated Querying
In the second variant, we designed ActiveLLM similar to other active learning strategies, allowing the LLM to query new instances repeatedly. For this, it is sensible to include data that was selected in previous iterations. These instances must be incorporated into the prompt in such a way that the prompt does not become too large. Therefore, we consider three additional prompt parameters:
• No Recap: Indicating the baseline, where no instances from previous queries are recalled.
• Recap: The instances from previous queries are directly included in the prompt.
•Index Recap: To reduce the context size, the indices of the instances from previous queries are included in the prompt.
For the latter two scenarios, we included instructions in the prompt to indicate that instances from previous iterations are incorporated. In both cases, we did not include the annotated labels, as our experiments showed that they were generally disregarded by the language models.
The corresponding prompts and further details on how these designs are implemented are provided in Appendix A.
4 Experiments
4.1 Models, Datasets, and Hyperparameter
As is common in research, our experiments simulate the active learning cycle. To do this, we take a subset of the training set (labels removed) for the respective task and allow the LLM to select a specified number of instances based on the prompt given in Section 3. These instances are then assigned the true labels as if they were annotated by a perfect annotator. For comparison, the baseline method involves random sampling, where the first instances from a shuffled dataset are selected. We train BERT models and compare their performance on the task’s test or validation sets.

Given that GPT-4 [ 18] is achieving top results across various benchmarks, we primarily employ this model in our experiments, starting with the prompt engineering phase. This phase begins by comparing different prompt configurations, as illustrated in Figure 1. We then evaluate the effects of varying batch sizes for the unlabeled data used in the prompts.

In the subsequent experiments outlined in Section 4.3, we demonstrate the general applicability of our ActiveLLM

In the final set of experiments, we explore the feedback variant of ActiveLLM, which iteratively selects instances using
GPT-4. First, we compare the different prompts to retrieve previously selected instances. Then we use the best setting to overcome the cold start problem of the conventional active learning method PE.
4.1.2 Datasets
For the initial prompt engineering experiments, we are interested in a less common dataset, as a LLM might be less biased by data leakage. We chose the Specialized CTI few-shot dataset [ 3], where Twitter posts are classified according to their relevance to specific CTI events. Detailed dataset specifications are available in Appendix B Table 4. In the general applicability tests, we used the best-performing prompt from earlier experiments on the commonly-used GLUE
benchmark, sampling 32 instances for each task to create a few-shot scenario. For comparing with other methods, we utilize the AGNews dataset [ 34] (32 instances), for which a SetFit adaptation exists. The SST-2 dataset [ 25], previously used in the general applicability section, is also employed in the final experiments.
4.1.3 Hyperparameters and Models
During the prompt engineering experiments, we aim to minimize random factors by generating five different randomizations of the CTI task and applying both the baseline and each prompt to these variations. For each evaluation, we select five different seeds for training the successor model, with the reported results for the baseline and each prompt being the average score from 25 runs (5 randomizations times 5 trained models). For subsequent experiments, we create one randomization of the datasets but continue to train five models and provide the average score.
As a successor model for the selected instances, we chose BERT [ 6]. While foundational works such as from Devlin et al. [6]typically train BERT-like models for only 3-5 epochs, other studies [ 8,16] suggest that higher epoch counts are necessary for stable and optimal performance in low-data regimes. Consequently, we have opted for 25 epochs for our experiments. Otherwise, we adhere to the default Huggingface transformer parameters, with a learning rate of 1e-3, a weight decay of 0.01, and a dropout probability of 0.1. For conventional active learning methods, we construct a model-matching scenario where the query model and the successor model are the same.
To underscore the practicality of our method, we employ chat versions of the LLMs to simulate realistic user interaction scenarios within a chat environment.

4.3 General Applicability
In this section, we aim to assess how ActiveLLM equipped with various LLMs and the prompt configuration B2 from
Section 4.2.1 perform on the more common datasets of the GLUE benchmark. Unlike previous experiments, we only sample one dataset for each task but continue to average the performance across five training runs.
The results of this experiment are given in Table 3. ActiveGPT4, ActiveGPT4o and ActiveMistralLarge are given 200unlabeled instances, as identified in the batch size experiments (Section 4.2.2). Llama 3, GPT-3.5, Gemini-Ultra and
Mixtral can not process such extensive prompts and are therefore given 100 instances. Notably, on average over all tasks
Mistral Large with 200 examples emerged as the superior model, even surpassing GPT-4. However, ActiveGPT4 is the only one that consistently improved results, while other variants decreased performance in at least one task compared to the baseline. Intriguingly, despite being the weakest overall, Gemini-Ultra achieved the best scores in MRPC and
COLA. Furthermore, although Mistral-Large with 200 examples generally outperformed its 100-example counterpart, it yielded inferior results in five out of eight tasks. The tasks in which Mistral-Large with 200 instances performs better are those with a generally shorter text length, suggesting that choosing a batch size based on token length may be better than a static batch size.
Overall, ActiveLLM proves to be a robust method for enhancing performance in few-shot scenarios. The most significant improvements were observed with ActiveMistralLarge (200) and ActiveGPT4 on WNLI and SST2, respectively, achieving improvements of 17.19 and 17.11 percentage points over the baseline.

4.3.1 Language Model Analysis
In addition to performance discrepancies among different LLMs on the GLUE benchmark, as detailed in Table 3, our analysis revealed distinct response patterns. GPT-4, GPT-4o and Mistral-Large have particularly high reliability and transparency. GPT-4o and Mistral Large typically provided concise answers, while all three models initially offered general advice on example selection before proceeding with more detailed responses. GPT-3.5 was also reliable but much more succinct in explaining its instance choices than GPT-4. Gemini-Ultra often refused to answer and indicated its limitations by stating it could not perform tasks beyond those typical of a language model. Mixtral struggled the most with task comprehension, frequently generating irrelevant instances based on the given data and overlooking the initial instructions.
While all models occasionally failed to select exactly 32 instances, this issue was least common with GPT-4, GPT-4o and Mistral-Large.

In our last experiments, we investigate how ActiveLLM performs in non-few-shot scenarios. For this purpose, we selected a sample size of 25 and evaluated the process up to 300 examples.
Figure 5 indicates that ActiveGPT4 is particularly beneficial in few-shot scenarios up to about 100 instances. Beyond this point, random sampling mostly matches its performance. Providing the model with the indices of these instances appears to be the most efficient method, as it does not overly complicate or significantly enlarge the prompt.
These results motivate the subsequent experiment in which we explore whether ActiveLLM might be a viable solution for overcoming the cold start problem of learning strategies.
4.5.2 Overcoming the Cold Start Problem
As outlined in Section 1, a pervasive issue in active learning research is the initial poor performance due to insufficient data, which leads to random or worse selections. However, as deep learning models increasingly excel in low-data regimes, the quality of initial data becomes progressively crucial. Observing that ActiveLLM excels in few-shot scenarios, we hypothesized that combining traditional active learning strategies with ActiveLLM could be beneficial in non-few-shot scenarios as well. Consequently, in this evaluation, we used the first 50 examples selected by ActiveGPT4
(index recap mode) as the starting point for PE.
Once again, a selection size of 25 was used, and the experiments continued until reaching a total of 300 examples The results in Figure 6 clearly show that using ActiveLLM as a starting point for PE outperforms PE with cold start until a plateau is reached at around 100 instances. This indicates that ActiveLLM is indeed an effective method for mitigating the cold start issues in active learning strategies.

5 Discussion & Conclusion
Utilizing instruction-tuned LLMs presents several advantages for active learning with pre-trained transformers like
BERT. Since these LLMs exhibit substantial zero-shot capabilities, they overcome the cold start problem and can be directly applied to a dataset. This is crucial for models like BERT, which already demonstrate strong performance in few-shot settings, rendering active learning strategies that face the cold start problem less useful. Realised in the
ActiveLLM method, we show that this procedure indeed excels in few-shot scenarios in contrast to other active learning strategies and even outperforms the state-of-the-art few-shot learning method SetFit. In further experiments, we also demonstrate a variant of ActiveLLM usable for non-few-shot scenarios incorporating feedback from previous iterations.
In a combined setting, we demonstrate that ActiveLLM overcomes the cold start problem inherent in conventional active learning strategies, exemplified by the PE active learning strategy.
Moreover, the LLMs do not need to be trained during the active learning process, making ActiveLLM especially suitable for practical scenarios. Even efficient methods need several minutes to perform one querying iteration [ 32].
While ActiveLLM considers only a small part of the unlabeled data, it achieves significant improvements and requires only a few seconds for querying. Furthermore, these LLMs are accessible through several chat interfaces, enabling practitioners from all domains without a deep machine learning or programming background to freely implement
ActiveLLM.
5.1 LLM-based Active Learning Strategies
The question remains as to how LLMs choose instances. It is nearly impossible to assess if the models truly perform the strategies they claim to execute. However, LLMs are known to be good at finding topics and patterns in texts, which could suggest that diversity sampling is a valid procedure. We expect these models are capable of identifying unique, representative, frequent, or highly information-dense instances. Similarly, we expect these models to perform well on the tasks themselves, which is why they could be relatively proficient at selecting a balanced set of all classes. However, these models cannot inspect their own internals, which means they certainly are not performing uncertainty sampling based on these internals. However, related to uncertainty, LLMs might be very well suited for identifying difficult or ambiguous examples. Occasionally, the models mention that they try to filter out any instances that are anomalies that would teach the successor model incorrect patterns. In the feedback variant, the language models often directly mention strategies regarding the feedback, explaining that for selecting the instances, they incorporate past instances and try to avoid redundancy, but also track very ambiguous instances from which more data might be needed in the successor training.

5.2 Limitations
In this study, we focus on active learning for BERT-like transformer models. While LLMs like GPT-4 may have greater capabilities, they are too expensive and resource-intensive to deploy in many cases, and APIs may be too prohibitive because of data privacy reasons. While BERT-like models remain common for classification tasks, we also look forward to using ActiveLLM on LLMs like GPT-4. As reported by [ 35], the selection of training examples for demonstration in the prompt can also have significant effects on the LLM performance, making ActiveLLM a promising method.
The context size is crucial for ActiveLLM. If the prompts get too large, the LLMs tend to forget the task or are not able to reason over the various instances. Although some models were reported to be able to handle long context inputs, we found that they were not able to reason perfectly over lengthy contexts. This is in line with the findings of Hsieh et al. [12] who argue that simple retrieval-based tests, like the common needle-in-a-haystack test, are not suitable for testing long context understanding. In our results, we noticed differences for tasks with longer instances on average. Therefore, we are interested in considering not only the instances as a length restriction but also the token count in future work.
Our work focuses on the utilization of the chat versions of the respective models to emphasize practicability. However, directly using the models, e.g., with the APIs, might yield different results. While this might only play an insignificant difference for Llama or Mixtral, some chat interfaces, like those from OpenAI or Google, hide parameters, preceding prompts, and exact version declarations. We see our results as a baseline that might be improved with different parameters or without a preceding prompt in the future.
Furthermore, we look forward to works further refining the prompt design. For example, the prompt could promote that the language model should favor certain active learning strategies. It would also be interesting to analyze certain strategies in isolation, like only diversity sampling. While we did not receive better results incorporating the true labels during iterated querying, this behavior might stem from the fact that we only evaluated ActiveLLM on at most three-class multi-classification tasks. We expect with a growing number of classes that the models identify to also look for the count of already labeled instances of a certain class.

A Full Prompt Design for Feedback Mode
Figure 7: Complete prompt design for the feedback mode of ActiveLLM, including the text of the ’advice’ parameter, which represents the description of several active learning strategies.
Figure 7 illustrates the comprehensive prompt design for the second variant of ActiveLLM. Compared to the prompt design of the first variant, this one contains the parameters ’No Recap’, ’Recap’ and ’Index Recap’. In scenarios ’No
Recap’ and ’Recap’, the unlabeled instances presented to the model are the ones immediately following the last labeled instance, extending up to the index of this last labeled instance plus the batch size, typically 200 instances. In contrast, the ’Index Recap’ scenario maintains the last presented instance but includes all preceding instances, allowing the model to access texts from the specific indices given as labelled. Additionally, Figure 7 introduces the ’advice’ parameter, which outlines various active learning strategies, including representativeness, diversity, difficulty, stratification, balance, temporal/special distribution, and bias avoidance.

B Dataset Details
The Spezialised CTI few-shot dataset consists of tweets that are binary labelled based on their relevance to cybersecurity experts during the Microsoft Exchange Server data breach in 2021. The dataset is split into a full and few-shot training and development set, with only the few-shot datasets being relevant in our case. The splits (train, dev) consist of 1800and 600 instances for the full set and 32 and 8 instances for the few-shot set, respectively. The test set is the same in both cases and consists of 601 instances. The details are also given in 4.
